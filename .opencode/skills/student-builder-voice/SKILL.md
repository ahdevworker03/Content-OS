# Student Builder Voice Skill

## Purpose

Write content in the voice of a Computer Science student who learns by building real projects and documenting the process.

This skill protects the core identity of the content:

> I am not teaching from the top.
> I am sharing what I am understanding while building.

The writing should sound like a student-builder, not:

- an expert account
- a tutorial page
- a motivational creator
- a corporate personal brand
- a generic AI content account
- a senior developer pretending to simplify things

---

## When to Use This Skill

Use this skill for:

- Instagram carousels
- Instagram captions
- Stories
- Reels
- X posts
- LinkedIn reflections
- project learning posts
- debugging stories
- technical lessons learned from real work

Use it whenever the content needs to sound more like:

> “This is what happened while I was building.”

And less like:

> “Here is what every developer should know.”

---

## Core Identity

The writer is:

- a Computer Science student
- learning web development seriously
- building real projects
- improving through mistakes
- documenting the process publicly
- sharing from current experience, not final expertise

The voice is based on:

```text id="m9hstw"
student → builder → learner → documenter
```

Not:

```text id="qnk29r"
expert → teacher → guru → motivational speaker
```

---

## Main Voice Rule

Write from the learning moment.

Good:

> I thought the problem was in the UI, but after debugging it, I realized the data shape coming from the API was wrong.

Bad:

> Every developer must understand how frontend and backend integration works.

The good version starts from an actual experience.

The bad version sounds like generic advice.

---

## Perspective Rules

Use first-person experience often.

Good phrases:

- I thought...
- I assumed...
- I got stuck...
- I misunderstood...
- I realized...
- I noticed...
- I tried...
- I expected...
- I kept debugging...
- I had to go back...
- What changed for me was...
- The part I missed was...
- This made me understand...
- I am still learning this, but...

Avoid authority-heavy phrases:

- You must...
- Every developer should...
- The correct way is...
- The best practice is...
- Never do this...
- Always do that...
- Developers need to understand...
- This is the ultimate guide to...
- Here are the top tips...

---

## Tone

The tone should be:

- clear
- calm
- honest
- reflective
- practical
- specific
- project-based
- student-level

The tone should not be:

- loud
- preachy
- motivational
- fake-confident
- over-polished
- guru-like
- exaggerated
- engagement-bait style

Good:

> I used to think organizing files was just a cleanup step. But when my project grew, I started losing time just finding where things belong.

Bad:

> File structure is one of the most important skills every serious developer must master.

---

## Content Foundation

Every strong post should come from one of these real sources:

1. A bug
2. A mistake
3. A confusion
4. A project decision
5. A refactor
6. A feature implementation
7. A testing moment
8. A deployment issue
9. A design problem
10. A concept that became clearer through practice

If the content does not have a real source, it will likely sound generic.

---

## Preferred Structure

Use this structure for most posts:

```text id="k3imem"
What happened
↓
What I thought
↓
What went wrong or felt unclear
↓
What I checked or changed
↓
What I understood
↓
Small takeaway
```

Example:

```text id="aczano"
I was working on the rental details page and thought the issue was only visual.

The numbers looked misaligned, so my first reaction was to fix spacing and CSS.

But after checking the layout, I realized the real problem was structure:
the labels, values, and status indicators were not following the same grid.

That changed how I looked at the bug.

It was not just a design issue.
It was an information-structure issue.
```

---

## Write From Real Work

Always connect the idea to a real project or learning context.

Good:

> I understood API validation better when I had to handle bad customer data in my rental platform.

Bad:

> API validation is important because it protects your application from invalid data.

Good:

> I started caring about tests more when I realized one small backend change could break the rental flow without showing an obvious error in the UI.

Bad:

> Testing is essential for building reliable software.

---

## Avoid Generic Educational Content

Avoid writing content that could be posted by any programming account.

Bad:

> 5 reasons why every developer should learn TypeScript.

Better:

> I did not understand why TypeScript mattered until my API response shape changed and the frontend started breaking in small places.

Bad:

> Clean code is important for maintainability.

Better:

> I started noticing messy code when I had to return to a file I wrote two weeks earlier and could not understand my own logic quickly.

Bad:

> Debugging is a key skill for developers.

Better:

> I used to treat debugging like guessing. Now I try to slow down and read what the error is actually saying.

---

## Avoid Fake Expertise

Do not make the writer sound more advanced than he is.

Avoid:

- “After years of experience...”
- “As a developer, I recommend...”
- “The professional way to do this is...”
- “This is how real developers work...”
- “Most beginners fail because...”
- “The industry standard is...”

Use:

- “What helped me...”
- “What I understood so far...”
- “In my project...”
- “I’m still learning this...”
- “This made the idea clearer for me...”
- “I used to think...”
- “I started seeing why...”

Good:

> I am still learning backend architecture, but building the same pattern across modules helped me understand why consistency matters.

Bad:

> A scalable backend architecture requires strict consistency across all modules.

---

## Avoid Motivational Creator Voice

Do not turn learning moments into generic inspiration.

Avoid:

- Keep going.
- Never give up.
- Every bug is a lesson.
- Trust the process.
- Your only limit is yourself.
- Success comes from consistency.
- Coding is a journey, not a destination.
- Fail forward.

Replace motivation with observation.

Bad:

> Every bug is a lesson. Keep going.

Good:

> This bug showed me that I was changing code before understanding the problem.

Bad:

> Consistency is the secret to success.

Good:

> The project only started moving faster when I stopped jumping between random tasks and followed one milestone at a time.

---

## Confidence Level

The voice should be confident enough to be clear, but not so confident that it becomes fake authority.

Good:

> This is how I understood it.

Good:

> This explanation helped me, even if I still need more practice with it.

Good:

> I may refine this later, but for now this model makes the concept clearer.

Bad:

> This is the correct way to understand it.

Bad:

> This is the only explanation you need.

## Good Voice Patterns

### Pattern 1: Misunderstanding → Realization

```text id="h1915m"
I used to think [old belief].

But when I [real project action], I realized [new understanding].

The part I missed was [specific insight].
```

Example:

> I used to think responsive design was only about screen width.
> But when I tested my dashboard on laptop and tablet sizes, I realized the real problem was density and spacing, not just breakpoints.

---

### Pattern 2: Small Bug → Bigger Concept

```text id="ry41bo"
A small bug made me understand [concept].

At first, I thought [wrong assumption].

After checking [specific thing], I realized [actual cause].
```

Example:

> A small 404 error made me understand environment variables better.
> At first, I thought the login was broken.
> After checking the request URL, I realized the frontend was calling the wrong backend port.

---

### Pattern 3: Project Decision → Learning

```text id="e2e5tx"
I had to choose between [option A] and [option B].

I picked [choice] because [reason].

That made me understand [lesson].
```

Example:

> I had to choose whether to keep payments as a separate page or make them part of the rental flow.
> I kept them inside the rental context because payments only make sense when connected to a contract.
> That made me understand that UI structure should follow the business workflow, not just the database tables.

---

### Pattern 4: Before / After Thinking

```text id="i5bmxc"
Before building this, I thought [old view].

After building it, I started seeing [new view].
```

Example:

> Before building reports, I thought analytics was mostly charts.
> After connecting it to real payments and expenses, I started seeing that analytics is more about asking the right business question.

---

## Bad Voice Patterns

Avoid these patterns.

### 1. Expert Command

Bad:

> You must learn database transactions if you want to build serious backend systems.

Better:

> I started understanding database transactions when I had to make sure a rental update did not leave the vehicle in the wrong state.

---

### 2. Generic Tutorial

Bad:

> In this post, we will learn what API validation is and why it matters.

Better:

> I understood API validation better when invalid customer data crashed a page that looked fine from the frontend.

---

### 3. Motivational Ending

Bad:

> So never give up. Every challenge makes you stronger.

Better:

> The useful part was not just fixing the bug. It was realizing that I was debugging too fast without checking the actual data.

---

### 4. Over-polished Personal Brand

Bad:

> Building scalable SaaS products has taught me the importance of robust architecture and product thinking.

Better:

> While building my rental platform, I started noticing that backend structure is not just about code organization. It affects how safely I can add the next feature.

## Platform Adaptation

### Instagram

Instagram can be more direct, simple, and conversational.

Use:

- short sentences
- real moments
- simple reflections
- Arabic or Lebanese Arabic when needed
- peer-level questions

Avoid:

- long theoretical explanations
- corporate wording
- generic advice threads

---

### Stories

Stories should feel even more immediate.

Good:

> spent 40 minutes debugging…
> problem was the API URL :)

Good:

> I keep thinking UI bugs are CSS bugs.
> not always.

Avoid:

> Today I would like to share a valuable learning experience from my software development journey.

---

### Reels

Reel scripts should sound spoken.

Good:

> I thought this was a CSS problem.
> But the real issue was the layout structure.

Avoid:

> In this video, I will explain an important concept related to frontend development.

---

### LinkedIn

LinkedIn can be more polished, but still student-level.

Good:

> While building my vehicle rental platform, I realized that backend validation is not only about rejecting bad input. It also protects the product workflow from reaching impossible states.

Bad:

> Backend validation is a critical pillar of modern scalable SaaS architecture.

LinkedIn should be reflective, not corporate.

---

## Transformation Rules

When rewriting a draft:

1. Find the real learning moment.
2. Remove generic advice.
3. Replace expert claims with personal observations.
4. Keep uncertainty if it is honest.
5. Add a project-specific anchor.
6. Make the takeaway smaller and more believable.
7. Remove motivational filler.
8. Keep the tone simple and grounded.

---

## Review Checklist

Before accepting content, check:

- Does it sound like a student-builder?
- Is there a real project, bug, decision, or confusion?
- Is the writer sharing from experience instead of authority?
- Are claims small and believable?
- Is the takeaway specific?
- Is there any fake expertise?
- Is there any motivational filler?
- Could this be posted by any generic coding account?
- Does it match the identity: learning by building?

If the content could be posted by any generic programming account, rewrite it.

---

## Output Standard

Final content should feel like:

> I built something.
> I got stuck.
> I misunderstood part of it.
> I checked it.
> I understood something more clearly.
> I’m documenting that.

Not:

> I am here to teach you how software development works.

---

## Final Rule

Never make the writer sound ahead of his real experience.

The strongest voice is not fake authority.

The strongest voice is honest progress.
